## Backpressure, Calibration and Parallel Analysis {#ch21}

A pipeline that is correct at its design rate still has to answer three questions that only appear in operation. What happens when data arrives faster than it can be processed? How do configuration values, such as calibration, change while data is flowing? And how is the analysis behind reconstruction parallelised without races or irreproducible results? This chapter takes them in turn, using measurements from the simulated pipeline of Chapter 19.

### Capacity is arithmetic first

Suppose, hypothetically, that a source offers 200,000 records a second during a 5 ms burst, and the consumer completes 150,000 a second. The backlog grows at 50,000 a second, so the burst adds about 250 records. Add the backlog that may already exist when the burst starts, the longest scheduling pause you have measured (Chapter 1), and a margin, and you have a buffer size you can defend. If arrivals exceed service *indefinitely*, no finite buffer helps: a bigger one only postpones the moment of loss and adds latency on the way.

Latency and capacity budgets interact through Little's law (Chapter 1): the time an item spends queued is the backlog divided by the service rate. At 100,000 items a second, a backlog of 10,000 means 100 ms of waiting. A queue that never overflows can still fail its latency requirement. Size buffers for the pauses you must absorb, then check what the full buffer costs in latency.

Memory is the third budget. 100,000 queued 16-byte hits are 1.6 MB of payload; in the example, each source ring holds 256 batches of 64 hits, about 256 KB, and the merger's per-source buffers 16,384 hits each. These are all allocated at start-up, which is what lets the data path run without allocation (Chapter 11).

### Overload policy is part of correctness

When a buffer is full, something has to give, and each choice has consequences downstream:

- **Block the producer.** Safe only if the producer can wait. Software can; detector hardware usually cannot, and blocking only moves the overflow into the front end's own buffer.
- **Drop the newest.** Keeps what is already queued; loses incoming data.
- **Drop the oldest.** Favours fresh data; breaks an existing sequence.
- **Overwrite silently.** Never acceptable: it hides the loss.
- **Enter a fault state.** Stop acquisition and say why. Sometimes the requirement.

The right choice depends on the requirements and on how the data will be interpreted. A lost hit in PET is not just a lost count: if loss depends on the count rate, it biases the measurement, which is why scanners correct for *dead time* and why the software must report its own losses to the stage that applies those corrections. Whatever the policy, count offered, accepted, rejected and processed items, and define when the counts must reconcile. At shutdown they must balance exactly; during acquisition, accepted equals processed plus queued plus in flight, but reading the terms as independent atomics can momentarily violate that, so assert conservation at quiescent points or from a coordinated snapshot (Chapter F6).

### Overload, measured

The example applies the design of Chapter 19: the sources drop and count, everything downstream applies backpressure. Two options force overload. `--rate=0` runs the simulated detector as fast as the source threads can go, which on the reference machine is faster than the sorter can keep up with:

```text
simulated 10000000 decays in 2.95 s: 14999655 hits (3.9 M hits/s through the pipeline)
accounting: generated 14999655 = sent 11503271 + dropped at source 3496384 (54631 batches)
            sequence gaps seen downstream: 3496384 (dropped + rejected = 3496384)
queue delay (source push -> sorter pop): ... p50=575 p90=11534335 p99=48234495 ... (ns)
books balance: yes
```

The pipeline processed 3.9 million hits a second and dropped 23% of what was offered, all of it at the sources, all of it counted, and all of it independently confirmed by the sequence numbers. The queue delay shows the backpressure: the median batch still waits half a microsecond, but whenever the rings fill, batches wait tens of milliseconds behind the full ring.

`--slow-analysis` instead makes the last stage slow, sleeping 50 µs every 64 coincidences. Now the analysis ring fills, the sorter waits for it, the source rings fill behind the sorter, and the sources drop:

```text
simulated 10000000 decays in 10.06 s: 14999655 hits (1.1 M hits/s through the pipeline)
accounting: generated 14999655 = sent 10816359 + dropped at source 4183296 (65364 batches)
queue delay (source push -> sorter pop): ... p50=30146559 ... (ns)
books balance: yes
```

The median batch now waits 30 ms: the rings are permanently full, and Little's law gives the wait directly from the ring size and the drain rate. The design held its promise: loss happened in one place, was counted, and was proved by an independent mechanism. What it did not do is protect the *coincidences* from the loss of their hits, and it cannot: a lost hit is a lost coincidence. The engineering response to sustained overload is not a cleverer queue but a faster stage, a lower offered rate, or a declared fault.

### Batching

Batches amortise per-item costs, one ring operation per 64 hits instead of per hit, at the price of waiting for a batch to fill. At high rates a batch fills quickly and the cost is negligible; at low rates the first hit in a batch can wait a long time, so real systems add a maximum dwell time that flushes a partial batch. The example's sources flush on every 64th hit only, which is fine for a simulator that is never idle and would not be for hardware. Evaluate batch sizes under the same offered load, recording throughput, latency percentiles, occupancy and drops, and expect the answer to be "the smallest batch that meets the throughput requirement with margin", not "the biggest".

### Calibration as an immutable snapshot

Raw energies must be converted to keV with per-module coefficients, and those coefficients change: after a calibration run, when temperature drifts, when a module is replaced. The rule from Chapter 19's configuration plane is that a change must be *consistent*: no batch may mix old and new coefficients, and every result must be traceable to the version that produced it.

`include/llds/pet/calibration.hpp` implements it with three pieces. A `CalibrationTable` is validated when it is built, every gain finite and positive and every offset finite, and is never modified afterwards. It carries a version number. A `CalibrationHolder` publishes the current table through `std::atomic<std::shared_ptr<const CalibrationTable>>`, the read-copy-update pattern of Chapter 12:

```cpp
class CalibrationHolder {
public:
    void publish(std::shared_ptr<const CalibrationTable> t) {
        if (!t) throw std::invalid_argument("calibration: null table");
        cur_.store(std::move(t), std::memory_order_release);
    }
    std::shared_ptr<const CalibrationTable> snapshot() const { return cur_.load(std::memory_order_acquire); }
private:
    std::atomic<std::shared_ptr<const CalibrationTable>> cur_;
};
```

And the data path takes one snapshot per batch, not per event: the analysis thread re-snapshots every 1,024 coincidences and processes the whole batch with that table. Chapter 12 measured `atomic<shared_ptr>` as the slowest of the read-side schemes, because each load updates a shared reference count; at one load per thousand events, that cost disappears. Put each cost at the frequency where it is affordable.

An invalid table never reaches the data path, because the constructor throws. A module number outside the table is a configuration mismatch that the decoder should have prevented; `energy_kev` returns NaN for it, and since NaN fails every comparison, no energy window accepts it (Chapter F4).

The example starts with a nominal calibration (version 1, every gain 1.0) while the simulated modules' true gains vary from 0.82 to 1.25, and the control thread publishes the corrected table (version 2) halfway through the run:

```text
calibration v1: 4382720 coincidences, 1567872 in the 425-650 keV window (35.8%)
calibration v2: 4388568 coincidences, 2201933 in the 425-650 keV window (50.2%)
```

With wrong gains, many photopeak photons land outside the 425–650 keV window and the acceptance drops from 50% to 36%. Because every count is tagged with its version, the effect is measurable and attributable, instead of being a mysterious dip in the middle of an acquisition. If raw records are kept, data taken with version 1 can be reprocessed later with version 2; that choice changes storage and provenance requirements and must be designed in, not added later.

### Parallel analysis behind reconstruction

Reconstruction itself is beyond this book, but its data-parallel structure is not. Iterative algorithms such as MLEM and OSEM repeatedly project an image estimate along every line of response and compare with the measured counts, and the work is dominated by two patterns that this book has covered: big reductions and histograms. Both raise the same hazards.

**Histograms.** Filling a histogram of counts per detector pair (the raw material of a sinogram) from many threads, with every thread incrementing shared bins, makes the bins the most contended cache lines in the program. Atomic increments are correct and slow; a lock per bin is slower. The standard answer is the one Exercise F5.1 used for a sum: give each thread a private histogram, have no thread touch another's, and merge after joining. Memory grows with the thread count, and the hot loop has no shared writes at all. Exercise 21.1 implements it for module pairs, with each private histogram on its own cache lines.

**Floating-point reductions.** Floating-point addition is not associative: `(a + b) + c` can differ from `a + (b + c)` in the last bits, and with values of very different magnitudes by much more. A parallel reduction groups terms differently from a serial one, and differently again with a different number of threads. So a mismatch between a parallel and a serial result is not automatically a race, and a match is not automatically correct. Decide in advance which property is required: *bitwise reproducibility* (the same input always produces the same bits, which a fixed partitioning and a fixed merge order provide, independent of scheduling) or *a bounded numerical error* (which needs an error budget derived before the experiment, not a tolerance picked after seeing a mismatch). In regulated software, "the result changes when the thread count changes" is a finding someone will ask about; the answer should be in the design, not discovered in testing.

Integer counts are exact, so the merged histogram of Exercise 21.1 must equal the serial one bit for bit for any thread count, and the test checks exactly that. Replace the counts with floating-point weights and it no longer holds, which is the point of the final exercise.

### Summary

- Size buffers by arithmetic: burst excess, longest pause, margin. Little's law turns backlog into latency.
- Overload policy is part of correctness. Choose it from the requirements; count every loss where it happens.
- Under sustained overload, a correct pipeline loses data in one place and proves it; only a faster stage or a lower rate removes the loss.
- Calibration is an immutable, validated, versioned snapshot, taken once per batch. Tag results with the version.
- Parallel histograms use private bins merged after join. Floating-point reductions need a stated reproducibility or error requirement.

### Exercises

1. **Size a buffer.** A source delivers 8 million hits a second; the sorter sustains 10 million a second but can be descheduled for up to 5 ms. How many hits must be buffered to survive one such pause without loss? How long does the backlog take to drain afterwards?

2. **Which policy?** For each, choose an overload policy and justify it: (a) a live display of count rates; (b) list-mode data for a quantitative scan; (c) an audit log of operator commands.

3. **Calibration race.** A colleague proposes updating gains in place with one `std::atomic<double>` per module, "so no snapshot is needed". Describe two things that go wrong.

4. **Snapshot frequency.** Measure, with `ch12_reclamation`, the cost of one `atomic<shared_ptr>` load on your machine. At what snapshot frequency would it reach 1% of a 100 ns per-coincidence budget?

5. **Hands-on: Exercise 21.1.** Implement `pair_histogram` in `exercises/ch21/pair_histogram.hpp` with private, cache-line-separated histograms merged in a fixed order. Then write a version that uses one shared array of `std::atomic<std::uint64_t>` and compare the two with 1 to 4 threads.

6. **Hands-on: reproducibility.** Change the histogram to accumulate `double` weights drawn from a range spanning six orders of magnitude. Compare serial, 2-thread and 4-thread results. Which are bitwise identical when you run each twice, and why?
