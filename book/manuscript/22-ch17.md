## Tuning the Platform {#ch17}

A data structure's latency is bounded below by its design and above by its platform. The SPSC ring of Chapter 8 transfers a message in a few tens of nanoseconds, but if the consumer thread is descheduled for a millisecond, that message waits a millisecond. Most of the tail latency that real systems show comes not from their data structures but from the operating system, the hardware and, in the cloud, the hypervisor. This chapter catalogues those sources, measures their effect on a small pipeline, and gives a step-by-step procedure for removing them.

### Where the tail comes from

Chapter 1 measured "jitter" on an idle thread: a loop that does nothing but read the clock still sees gaps of tens of microseconds to milliseconds. Those gaps are the platform taking the core away. The usual suspects, roughly in order of how often they matter:

- **Scheduler preemption.** Another runnable thread, or a kernel thread, is given your core. On a busy machine, this is milliseconds.
- **Interrupts.** Network cards, disks, timers and inter-processor interrupts (IPIs) run handlers on whatever core they are routed to, followed by **softirq** processing (network receive, timers, RCU callbacks), which can take tens of microseconds.
- **The scheduler tick.** By default every core takes a timer interrupt 100 to 1,000 times a second, for accounting and load balancing: a few microseconds each time.
- **Kernel threads**: `kworker` workqueues, RCU callback threads, `ksoftirqd`, `kcompactd` and `khugepaged` (memory compaction for transparent huge pages).
- **Power management.** A core that has gone idle enters a deep **C-state** to save power, and waking it takes from a few to over a hundred microseconds. Frequency scaling (**P-states**) changes how fast the core runs, so the same code takes different times.
- **SMT siblings.** Two hyperthreads on one physical core share its execution units and L1 and L2 caches; a busy sibling slows your thread by up to half.
- **Memory management.** Page faults (Chapter 11), TLB shootdowns (an IPI to every core running the process when any thread unmaps memory), swap, and THP compaction.
- **NUMA.** Memory allocated on the other socket costs about 1.5 to 2 times as much to reach.
- **Firmware.** System management interrupts (SMIs) stop *all* cores for tens to hundreds of microseconds, invisibly to the OS.
- **The hypervisor**, in a virtual machine: the host can deschedule your virtual CPU (**steal time**), and its own interrupts and housekeeping are invisible to the guest.

### Measurements

`examples/ch17_tuning.cpp` builds a three-stage pipeline, feed to work to sink, connected by two SPSC rings. The feed injects a timestamped event every 2 µs, a steady 500,000 events per second, and the sink records each event's end-to-end latency. It runs four configurations of a million events each:

- **A**: threads unpinned; consumers *sleep* for 50 µs when they find their ring empty. This is the "polite" design that most general-purpose code uses.
- **B**: threads unpinned; consumers *yield* the CPU when idle.
- **C**: threads unpinned; consumers *busy-spin*.
- **D**: threads pinned to cores 0, 1 and 2; consumers busy-spin.

On the reference machine, a four-vCPU cloud VM with no tuning at all:

```text
                                   p50       p99       p99.9         max   (ns)
A unpinned, sleep when idle     116,735   260,095   1,490,943   2,384,155
B unpinned, yield when idle         807    18,431   1,032,191   2,095,131
C unpinned, busy-spin               543     5,247     200,703   1,032,659
D pinned, busy-spin                 543   102,399     974,847   2,585,359
```

**Sleeping when idle costs two hundred times the median.** A 50 µs sleep is actually longer: Linux adds a default **timer slack** of 50 µs to every sleep, so the thread wakes 50 to 100 µs later, and then the scheduler must find it a core. Meanwhile, events arrive every 2 µs and queue up, so the median event waits 117 µs. A sleeping consumer is the right choice for a background task and the wrong one for anything on a latency-critical path.

**Yielding is much better at the median** (807 ns) because the thread stays runnable: `sched_yield` gives the core away only if another thread wants it. But when one does, the consumer may wait a full scheduler time slice, which shows up in the p99 of 18 µs.

**Busy-spinning gives the best median** (543 ns for two ring transfers and the work stage, on a virtual machine) and the best p99, 5 µs. Its p99.9 of 200 µs and its maximum of 1 ms are the platform: on a shared VM, spinning threads are still preempted.

**Pinning made the tail worse.** This is the most instructive result in the table. Pinning the three threads to cores 0, 1 and 2, without doing anything else, raised the p99 from 5 µs to 102 µs. Pinned threads cannot move. When the kernel needs to run something on core 0 (a kernel thread, an interrupt, the benchmark's own housekeeping, or another process), the pinned thread must wait for it. An unpinned thread is migrated by the scheduler to the fourth, idle core. **Pinning without isolation is a pessimisation**: it removes the scheduler's freedom to avoid interference without removing the interference. Pinning pays off only when the pinned cores are also emptied of everything else, which is what the rest of this chapter does.

On a properly isolated bare-metal machine, configuration D typically shows a p99.9 within a few hundred nanoseconds of its median, and a maximum of a few microseconds. The difference between those numbers and the ones above is entirely platform.

### Wait strategies

A consumer that finds its queue empty has four options, and Chapter 9's wait strategies combine them:

| Strategy | Wake-up latency | CPU cost while idle | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Busy-spin | Tens of nanoseconds | One full core | Critical-path threads on isolated cores |
| Spin with `pause` | Tens of nanoseconds | One core, less power, kinder to an SMT sibling | Default spin loop |
| Yield | Microseconds, sometimes a time slice | Low if others want the core | Shared cores, moderate latency |
| Block (futex, condition variable, `atomic::wait`) | 5 to 50 µs or more | None | Background threads, rare events |
| Spin, then block | Short when busy, slow after idling | Bounded | Bursty traffic with long idle periods |

The `pause` instruction (`llds::cpu_relax()`) tells the core that it is in a spin loop. It reduces power, avoids a pipeline flush when the loop exits, and gives an SMT sibling more of the core. Its duration varies by microarchitecture: about 10 cycles on older Intel cores, about 140 on Skylake and later, which can make spin loops that count iterations behave differently on different machines. Newer cores add `umwait` and `tpause` (Intel) and `mwaitx` (AMD), which wait in a light power state until a cache line changes or a deadline arrives; they are a good fit for spin loops that may wait a long time.

The hybrid **spin-then-park** strategy spins for a bounded time (typically tens of microseconds, enough to cover the inter-arrival time during bursts), then blocks. It is the best general-purpose choice, and it is what most high-performance mutexes do internally (Chapter 5).

### Isolating cores

The goal of isolation is that the cores running the critical path run *nothing else*: no other processes, no kernel threads, no interrupts, and ideally not even the scheduler tick. Linux provides a set of controls that together achieve this. On a machine with cores 0 to 15, reserving 4 to 7 for the critical path, the kernel command line would contain:

```text
isolcpus=managed_irq,domain,4-7 nohz_full=4-7 rcu_nocbs=4-7 irqaffinity=0-3
```

- **`isolcpus=domain,4-7`** removes the cores from the scheduler's load balancing: no thread runs on them unless explicitly placed there with `taskset`, `sched_setaffinity` or a cpuset. The `managed_irq` flag also keeps managed device interrupts off them where possible. The kernel documentation has at times described `isolcpus` as deprecated in favour of cgroup v2 cpuset partitions (`cpuset.cpus.partition = isolated`), which can be changed at run time; both are widely used.
- **`nohz_full=4-7`** stops the scheduler tick on those cores whenever they run only one task. A busy-spinning thread on a `nohz_full` core takes, ideally, no timer interrupts at all.
- **`rcu_nocbs=4-7`** moves the kernel's RCU callback processing off those cores, to kernel threads that run on the housekeeping cores.
- **`irqaffinity=0-3`** sets the default interrupt affinity to the housekeeping cores. Existing interrupts can be moved at run time by writing a CPU mask to `/proc/irq/N/smp_affinity`; stop the `irqbalance` daemon first, or it will move them back.

Some kernel housekeeping still needs explicit placement. Unbound workqueues obey `/sys/devices/virtual/workqueue/cpumask`; set it to the housekeeping cores. Check what is left with `ps -eLo psr,comm` (which threads are on which core) and by diffing `/proc/interrupts` over a few seconds: on a well-isolated core, the counters barely move.

Isolate *physical* cores, not hyperthreads. If core 4 and core 12 are SMT siblings, either isolate both and leave one idle, or disable SMT (`nosmt` on the command line, or `/sys/devices/system/cpu/smt/control`). `lscpu -e` and `/sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu*/topology/thread_siblings_list` show the pairing.

### Power and frequency

- **Fix the frequency.** Set the CPU frequency governor to `performance` (`cpupower frequency-set -g performance`), so the core does not ramp up and down. Some deployments also disable turbo for consistency: a core that turbos when its neighbours are idle and slows when they are busy produces run-to-run variation.
- **Limit C-states.** Deep idle states save power and cost wake-up time. A busy-spinning core never enters them, but housekeeping cores and blocking threads do. The C-states available can be limited with `intel_idle.max_cstate=1` or `processor.max_cstate=1` on the command line, or at run time by holding `/dev/cpu_dma_latency` open with a value of 0 written to it, which is what `tuned`'s `latency-performance` profile does.
- **Check for SMIs.** `hwlatdetect` (from `rt-tests`) or the kernel's `hwlat` tracer spins with interrupts disabled and reports gaps that only firmware can cause. If they are present, the BIOS settings (power management, memory error reporting, USB legacy emulation) are the only cure.

### Memory

- **Lock memory** with `mlockall(MCL_CURRENT | MCL_FUTURE)` so that nothing is paged out, and **pre-fault** every buffer, pool and stack before the critical path starts (Chapter 11).
- **Huge pages.** Explicit huge pages (`MAP_HUGETLB`, or a `hugetlbfs` mount) cut TLB misses for large structures; the step in Chapter 2's latency curve, where the working set outgrew the TLB's reach of 8 MiB, moves out to gigabytes with 2 MiB pages. Transparent huge pages are convenient, but their background compaction can stall: set them to `madvise` and request them only for chosen regions.
- **NUMA.** On multi-socket machines, run critical threads and their memory on the same node: `numactl --cpunodebind=1 --membind=1`, or allocate from the thread that will use the memory (Linux's first-touch policy) after pinning it. Keep the network card's node in mind too.
- **Avoid TLB shootdowns.** `munmap`, `mprotect` and `madvise(MADV_DONTNEED)` in any thread of the process send IPIs to every core running another of its threads, including your isolated ones. Allocators that return memory to the kernel do this. Pre-allocate, and configure the allocator not to trim (`mallopt(M_TRIM_THRESHOLD, ...)` for glibc).

### Scheduling policy and PREEMPT_RT

Threads on isolated cores run alone, so their scheduling policy hardly matters. Threads that must share cores with others, such as a control thread or a logger, can be given a real-time policy, `SCHED_FIFO` with a priority, so that they preempt ordinary threads immediately. Two warnings apply. A busy-spinning `SCHED_FIFO` thread never yields, so on a non-isolated core it starves every lower-priority thread, including kernel threads that the system needs, and the machine can appear to hang; the kernel's **RT throttling** (`/proc/sys/kernel/sched_rt_runtime_us`, 95% by default) exists to limit the damage. And a real-time priority does not help against interrupts or against non-preemptible sections in the kernel.

That last point is what **PREEMPT_RT** addresses. In a standard kernel, some code paths run with preemption disabled for long stretches, so even the highest-priority thread can wait hundreds of microseconds. PREEMPT_RT, merged into the mainline kernel in version 6.12 after two decades out of tree, makes almost all kernel code preemptible, turns interrupt handlers into schedulable threads and replaces most spinlocks with priority-inheriting mutexes. The result is bounded worst-case scheduling latency, typically tens of microseconds, at a small cost in throughput.

Whether you need it depends on the design. A system whose critical threads busy-spin on isolated cores never asks the scheduler for anything and gains little. A system whose critical threads *block* and must wake on time, such as an audio engine, an industrial controller, or the alarm path of a patient monitor, needs bounded wake-up latency, and PREEMPT_RT is the standard way to get it on Linux. `cyclictest` from `rt-tests`, which measures how late a periodic thread wakes, is the standard benchmark, and the `rtla` tools (`rtla timerlat`, `rtla osnoise`) show where the remaining latency comes from.

### Virtual machines

Every measurement in this book was taken on a cloud VM, and this chapter's table shows why that matters. A guest cannot isolate itself from the host: the hypervisor may deschedule a virtual CPU at any time (visible as `st`, steal time, in `top` and in `/proc/stat`), and host interrupts and housekeeping run on the physical cores behind the guest's virtual ones. For latency-critical production work in the cloud, use bare-metal instances or dedicated hosts, choose instance types that pin virtual CPUs to physical cores, and measure the platform with Chapter 1's jitter tool before measuring your code.

### A tuning procedure

Tuning is an experiment, not a checklist. Apply one change at a time, measure the full latency distribution after each (Chapter 4: percentiles, with coordinated omission corrected), and keep only the changes that help.

1. **Measure the baseline**: the application's end-to-end latency under realistic load, and the platform's jitter with the Chapter 1 tool on each core.
2. **Fix the obvious**: `performance` governor, `irqbalance` stopped, swap off or `vm.swappiness=0`, THP set to `madvise`.
3. **Choose the core layout**: which threads are on the critical path, which physical cores they get, which cores are for housekeeping. Keep SMT siblings of critical cores idle.
4. **Isolate**: `isolcpus` or cpuset partitions, `nohz_full`, `rcu_nocbs`, IRQ affinity and the workqueue mask. Reboot, and verify with `/proc/interrupts` and `ps`.
5. **Pin** critical threads to their isolated cores at start-up, by reading the isolated set (`/sys/devices/system/cpu/isolated`) rather than hard-coding numbers. Exercise 17.1 builds the parser for that file's format.
6. **Prepare memory**: `mlockall`, pre-fault, huge pages, NUMA binding.
7. **Choose wait strategies**: busy-spin on isolated cores, spin-then-park elsewhere.
8. **Verify** with tracing (`perf sched`, `rtla osnoise`, `perf stat -e context-switches,cpu-migrations,page-faults`): the critical threads should show no context switches, no migrations and no page faults after start-up.
9. **Monitor in production.** Configuration drifts: a kernel upgrade, a new daemon, a changed BIOS setting. Have the application check its own environment at start-up (are its cores isolated, is the governor right, is THP off) and record its latency histogram continuously.

Network-facing systems add one more layer, which is outside this book's scope: **kernel bypass** (DPDK, AF_XDP, or vendor libraries such as AMD's Onload and ef_vi) delivers packets directly to a user-space thread polling the network card, removing interrupts and the kernel's network stack from the receive path altogether.

### Summary

- Most tail latency comes from the platform: preemption, interrupts, ticks, kernel threads, power states, SMT, memory management, firmware and hypervisors.
- Measured on the untuned reference VM: sleeping when idle gave a 117 µs median; yielding, 807 ns; busy-spinning, 543 ns with a 5 µs p99.
- Pinning without isolation made the p99 twenty times worse (102 µs), because pinned threads cannot escape interference. Pin only onto isolated cores.
- Isolate with `isolcpus` or cpuset partitions, `nohz_full`, `rcu_nocbs`, IRQ affinity and the workqueue mask. Isolate physical cores.
- Fix frequency, limit C-states, check for SMIs, lock and pre-fault memory, use huge pages, respect NUMA, avoid TLB shootdowns.
- Use PREEMPT_RT when critical threads block and must wake on time; it adds little to a busy-spinning design.
- Tune one change at a time, measuring the full distribution, and monitor the configuration in production.

2. **Queueing.** In configuration A, events arrive every 2 µs. If the consumer sleeps for 100 µs and then drains the ring instantly, what is the latency distribution of the events that arrived during the sleep? Compare it with the measured p50 and p99.

Your system has a feed handler, a strategy thread, an order sender, a logger and a monitoring agent. Assign them to cores, say which cores are isolated, and where the card's interrupts go.

5. **RT starvation.** A developer gives a busy-spinning thread `SCHED_FIFO` priority 99 on a core that is not isolated, and the machine becomes unresponsive for seconds at a time. Explain what is happening and two ways to fix it.
